\begin{abstract}
This paper studies whether language-model safety rules can be formalized as linguistic smart contracts: vow-like control objects that combine natural-language intent with machine-checkable trigger conditions and binding consequences. The central mathematical claim is that prompt-visible rules are insufficient unless backed by explicit condition-consequence semantics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Formal Setup}
Let $q$ be a query, $p$ a policy clause, $C(q,a)$ a trigger predicate, and $K(a,q)$ a consequence map. Then the contract decision is
\[
d(q,p)=
\begin{cases}
K(a,q), & C(q,a)=1,\\
\mathrm{allow}, & C(q,a)=0.
\end{cases}
\]

\section{Constrained Objective}
Let the unconstrained action be
\[
a^*=\arg\max_a U(a\mid q).
\]
Under a vow-like constraint, the feasible set becomes
\[
F(q,p)=\{a : C(q,a)=0\},
\]
and the constrained action is
\[
a_c=\arg\max_{a\in F(q,p)} U(a\mid q).
\]
If no feasible action exists, the consequence function applies a safe fallback.

\section{Auxiliary Risk Model}
Let
\[
z(q)=[k(q),o(q),r(q),t(q)],
\]
and define
\[
s(q)=w_k k(q)+w_o o(q)+w_r r(q)+w_t t(q).
\]
A thresholded implementation uses
\[
d(q,p)=\mathrm{deny}\;\text{if}\; s(q)\ge \tau,\;\text{else allow}.
\]

\section{Evaluation}
Let $TP, TN, FP, FN$ carry their usual meanings. Then
\[
ASR=\frac{FN}{TP+FN},
\quad
Precision_{\mathrm{deny}}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},
\quad
Recall_{\mathrm{deny}}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
\]
The benchmark implemented in \texttt{run\_contract\_benchmark.py} compares a prompt-only baseline to a vow-style external contract layer.

\section{Assumptions}
This study assumes that trigger conditions can be defined with enough stability to be monitored externally, that attack success rate is an appropriate first-order robustness metric, and that matched benign prompts can expose the cost of stricter enforcement.

\section{Formal Claim Structure}

\subsection{Definition}
A linguistic smart contract is a policy object with an explicit trigger condition and a binding consequence that is executed independently of the model's willingness to comply.

\subsection{Proposition J1}
Explicit trigger-consequence semantics reduce attack success rate relative to prompt-only rules.

\subsection{Test Statistic for J1}
Compare $ASR$ between a prompt-only baseline and a contract-enforced system on matched attack families.

\subsection{Decision Rule for J1}
Support J1 only if the contract system yields a lower $ASR$ without collapsing benign-task acceptance.

\subsection{Null Hypothesis for J1}
Explicit trigger-consequence semantics do not improve attack success rate relative to prompt-only rules.

\subsection{Effect-Size Target for J1}
The target is a robust reduction in $ASR$ that persists under paraphrase and stronger attack families while preserving acceptable benign throughput.

\subsection{Control Condition for J1}
Compare against a prompt-only baseline and, where possible, a simpler keyword-filter baseline.

\subsection{Confound Checklist for J1}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item attack prompts overly matched to the explicit trigger vocabulary,
\item benign prompts too easy,
\item evaluation restricted to one attack family,
\item hidden reliance on shallow keyword filters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Mode for J1}
J1 fails if the measured $ASR$ improvement disappears under paraphrase or stronger attack variants.

\subsection{Proposition J2}
Explicit trigger semantics reduce paraphrase sensitivity of policy behavior.

\subsection{Test Statistic for J2}
Measure decision consistency across semantically equivalent rewordings of policy-relevant prompts.

\subsection{Decision Rule for J2}
Support J2 only if trigger activation and final decisions remain stable under paraphrase families.

\subsection{Null Hypothesis for J2}
Explicit trigger semantics do not reduce policy sensitivity to semantic rewording.

\subsection{Effect-Size Target for J2}
The target is a measurable increase in decision consistency across paraphrase-equivalent prompts.

\subsection{Control Condition for J2}
Use paraphrase sets spanning lexical, syntactic, and discourse-level variations with preserved meaning.

\subsection{Confound Checklist for J2}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item paraphrases that subtly alter meaning,
\item annotation errors in paraphrase equivalence,
\item inconsistent trigger parsing,
\item benchmark prompts too narrow to test semantic robustness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Mode for J2}
J2 fails if small rewordings produce inconsistent trigger activation or consequence selection.

\subsection{Proposition J3}
Externalized consequence execution reduces rule erosion under jailbreak pressure.

\subsection{Test Statistic for J3}
Compare denial precision, denial recall, and consequence-execution consistency between prompt-only and externally enforced variants.

\subsection{Decision Rule for J3}
Support J3 only if externalized enforcement preserves policy decisions under direct and indirect attack prompts.

\subsection{Null Hypothesis for J3}
Externalized consequence execution does not reduce rule erosion beyond simpler enforcement alternatives.

\subsection{Effect-Size Target for J3}
The target is a measurable gain in denial precision and denial recall under adversarial pressure without overwhelming benign false positives.

\subsection{Control Condition for J3}
Compare against prompt-only rules and lightweight filters under the same attack/benign split.

\subsection{Confound Checklist for J3}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item artificially easy attack detection,
\item excessive benign denial rates,
\item consequence rules that merely restate the benchmark labels,
\item missing indirect-attack coverage.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Mode for J3}
J3 fails if the external mechanism behaves like a shallow keyword filter or produces excessive benign false positives.

\section{Falsifiers}
The theory is weakened if trigger behavior is unstable under rewording, if attack success rate does not improve, or if benign false positives become too large to justify the gain in robustness.

\section{Evidence and Proof Standard}
Evidence means measurable robustness gains against controlled attack families. Strong evidence requires gains that survive paraphrase, stronger attacks, and benign-utility checks. Proof, in the empirical sense, requires persistent superiority over prompt-only rules without a simpler explanation such as trivial keyword blocking alone.
